# Regular Languages
The following statements are equivalent:
- $L$ is a regular language
- There exists a DFA $M$ with $L(M)=L$
- There exists a NFA $M$ with $L(M)=L$
- There exists a right-linear grammar $G$ with $L(G)=L$
- There exists a left-linear grammar $G$ with $L(G)=L$
- There exists a regular expression $r$ with $L(r)=L$
---
- If $L_{1},L_{2},L$ are regular languages, then also $$L_{1}\cup L_{2}\qquad L_{1}\cap L_{2}\qquad L_{1}L_{2}\qquad \bar L\qquad L_{1}\setminus L_{2}\qquad L^{*}\qquad L^{R}$$
## DFA
- **Minimal DFA**: The minimal DFA accepting a given language $L$ is unique up to isomorphism (renaming of the states). Two DFAs accept the same language if and only if their minimal DFAs *coincide* up to isomorphism (renaming of the states).
## NFA
- For every NFA $A$ there exists some DFA $B$ such that $L(A)=L(B)$
- **Minimization**: Minimizing of NFAs is PSpace-complete.
- If $L$ is regular, then its reverse $L^{R}$ is regular
## Grammar
- A language $L$ is generated by a right-linear grammar $\iff$ $L^{R}$ is generated by a left-linear grammar.
## Proofs
### Closure under Complements
Let $L$ be regular. Then there exists a DFA $M=(Q,\Sigma, \delta, q_{0}, F)$ with $L(M)=L$.
We define a DFA $N$ by $N=(Q,\Sigma, \delta, q_{0}, {\color{#F08}Q\setminus F})$. We show that $L(N)=\overline L$. For this purpose, let $w\in \Sigma^{*}$ be an arbitrary word. As the automata are deterministic, there is precisely one path starting at $q_{0}$ labelled with $w$.
Thus, there is precisely one state $q_{0}$ with $q_{0}\xrightarrow{w}q$.
We have $$w\in L \iff w\in L(M) \iff q\in F$$
Consequently $$w\in \overline L \iff w \in \overline{L(M)} \iff q\in (Q\setminus F) \iff w \in L(N)$$
Hence $L(N)=\overline L$

#### Proof using Right-Linear Grammars

Let $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ be regular languages. We show that $L_{1} \cup L_{2}$ is generated by a right-linear grammar and hence regular.

Since $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ are regular, there exist right-linear grammars $G_{1} = \left(\right. V_{1} , \Sigma , S_{1} , P_{1} \left.\right)$ and $G_{2} = \left(\right. V_{2} , \Sigma , S_{2} , P_{2} \left.\right)$ with $L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) = L_{1}$ and $L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right) = L_{2}$.

Without loss of generality, assume that $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ have no variables in common, that is, $V_{1} \cap V_{2} = \emptyset$. This can always be achieved by renaming the variables of one of the grammars.

Let $S$ be a fresh variable, that is, $S$ is neither in $V_{1}$ nor in $V_{2}$. Define a grammar $G = \left(\right. V , \Sigma , S , P \left.\right)$ by

- $V = V_{1} \cup V_{2} \cup \left\{\right. S \left.\right\}$, and
- $P = P_{1} \cup P_{2} \cup \left\{\right. S \rightarrow S_{1} , S \rightarrow S_{2} \left.\right\}$.

So $G$ is the combination of $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ with a fresh start variable $S$, and additional production rules $S \rightarrow S_{1}$ and $S \rightarrow S_{2}$.

Then a word $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$ is generated by $S$ if and only if it is generated by $S_{1}$ or $S_{2}$.

Thus $L \left(\right. G \left.\right) = L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) \cup L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right) = L_{1} \cup L_{2}$, and hence $L_{1} \cup L_{2}$ is a regular language.
### Closure under Concatenation
#### Proof using Regular Expressions
Let $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ be regular languages. We show that $L_{1}L_{2}$ is generated by a regular expression and hence regular.
Since $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ are regular, there exists regular expressions $r_{1}$ and $r_{2}$ with $L(r_{1})=L_{1}$ and $L(r_{2})=L_{2}$.
Then $r_{1}r_{2}$ is a regular expression. By definition $L(r_{1}r_{2})=L_{1}L_{2}$. Hence $L_{1}L_{2}$ is a regular language.
#### Proof using Right-Linear Grammars

Let $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ be regular languages. We show that $L_{1} L_{2}$ is generated by a right-linear grammar and hence regular.

Since $L_{1}$ and $L_{2}$ are regular, there exist right-linear grammars $G_{1} = \left(\right. V_{1} , \Sigma , S_{1} , P_{1} \left.\right)$ and $G_{2} = \left(\right. V_{2} , \Sigma , S_{2} , P_{2} \left.\right)$ with $L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) = L_{1}$ and $L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right) = L_{2}$.

We rename the variables such that $G_{1}$ and $G_{2}$ have no variables in common ($V_{1} \cap V_{2} = \emptyset$).

Define a grammar $G = \left(\right. V , \Sigma , S_{1} , P \left.\right)$ where

- $V = V_{1} \cup V_{2}$, and
- $P = P_{1}^{'} \cup P_{2}$ where $P_{1}^{'}$ is obtained from $P_{1}$ by replacing every rule of the shape

$$$
A \rightarrow w
$$$

with $A \in V_{1}$ and $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$ by

$$$
A \rightarrow w S_{2}
$$$

We will argue that $L \left(\right. G \left.\right) = L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right) = L_{1} L_{2}$.

As $G_{1}$ is a right-linear grammar, all its production rules are of the form

1. $A \rightarrow w B$, or
2. $A \rightarrow w$

for variables $A , B \in V_{1}$ and terminal words $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$.

As a consequence, every derivation in $G_{1}$ of a terminal word $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$ must be of the form:

$$$
V_{1} \Rightarrow w_{1} A_{1} \Rightarrow w_{2} A_{2} \Rightarrow \cdots \Rightarrow w_{n} A_{n} \Rightarrow w
$$$

for some $A_{1} , \ldots , A_{n} \in V_{1}$ and $w_{1} , \ldots , w_{n} \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$. The last rule application is of the shape (2) and all others are of shape (1).

Then, by the definition of $P_{1}^{'}$, we have for every $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$:

$$$
w \in L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) \quad \iff \quad V_{1} \left(\Rightarrow\right)^{*} w \text{ in } P_{1} \quad \iff \quad V_{1} \left(\Rightarrow\right)^{*} w S_{2} \text{ in } P_{1}^{'}
$$$

Every derivation of a terminal word $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$ in $G$ must be of the form:

$$$
V_{1} \Rightarrow_{P_{1}^{'}}^{*} w_{1} S_{2} \Rightarrow_{P_{2}}^{*} w_{1} w_{2}
$$$

for some $w_{1} , w_{2} \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$. The reason is that every right-hand sides of a rule in $P_{1}^{'}$ contain precisely one variable from $V_{1} \cup S_{2}$. So, the only way to derive a terminal word is to derive a word with $S_{2}$ from which point onward only rules of $P_{2}$ are applicable. Thus

$$$
V_{1} \Rightarrow_{P_{1}^{'}}^{*} w_{1} S_{2} \Rightarrow_{P_{2}}^{*} w_{1} w_{2} \quad \iff \quad w_{1} \in L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) \text{ and } w_{2} \in L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right)
$$$

Hence $L \left(\right. G \left.\right) = L \left(\right. G_{1} \left.\right) L \left(\right. G_{2} \left.\right) = L_{1} L_{2}$, and consequently $L_{1} L_{2}$ is a regular language.
### Closure under Reversal
#### Proof using Automata (NFAs)

Since $L$ is a regular language, there exists a NFA $M = \left(\right. Q , \Sigma , \left(\delta\right)_{M} , I_{M} , F_{M} \left.\right)$ with $L \left(\right. M \left.\right) = L$.

**Idea**:

- turn around all arrows, and
- swap initial and final states.

We define a NFA $N = \left(\right. Q , \Sigma , \left(\delta\right)_{N} , I_{N} , F_{N} \left.\right)$ where

- $I_{N} = F_{M}$,
- $F_{N} = I_{M}$, and
- the transition function is given by

$$
q \overset{a}{\rightarrow} q^{'} \text{ is a transition in } N \Longleftrightarrow q^{'} \overset{a}{\rightarrow} q \text{ is a transition in } M
$$

for all $q , q^{'} \in Q$ and $a \in \left(\right. \Sigma \cup \left\{\right. \lambda \left.\right\} \left.\right)$. Formally, that is

$$
\left(\delta\right)_{N} \left(\right. q , a \left.\right) = \left\{\right. q^{'} \in Q \mid q \in \left(\delta\right)_{M} \left(\right. q^{'} , a \left.\right) \left.\right\}
$$

for all $q \in Q$ and $a \in \left(\right. \Sigma \cup \left\{\right. \lambda \left.\right\} \left.\right)$.

As we have turned around the arrows, a forward path in $M$ becomes is a reversed path in $N$. Thus we have

$$
( * ) q \overset{w}{\rightarrow} q^{'} \text{ in } M \Longleftrightarrow q^{'} \overset{w^{R}}{\rightarrow} q \text{ in } N
$$

for all words $w$ and states $q , q^{'} \in Q$.

This property is intuitively obvious. Formally, this can be proven by induction on the length $n$ of the path $q \overset{w}{\rightarrow} q^{'}$. As proofs for the directions $\Rightarrow$ and $\Leftarrow$ are analogous, we only prove $\Rightarrow$:

- For the base case, length $0$, there is nothing to be shown.
- For the induction step $n \rightarrowtail n + 1$, we consider a path $q \overset{w}{\rightarrow} q^{'}$ of length $n + 1$ in $M$.  
Let $q \overset{\alpha}{\rightarrow} m$ with $\alpha \in \left(\right. \Sigma \cup \left\{\right. \lambda \left.\right\} \left.\right)$ be the first transition on this path.  
Then $w = \alpha v$ for some $v$ and the path is of the form $q \overset{\alpha}{\rightarrow} m \overset{v}{\rightarrow} q^{'}$.  
By induction hypothesis we obtain $q^{'} \overset{v^{R}}{\rightarrow} m$ in $N$ and by definition of $\left(\delta\right)_{N}$ we have $m \overset{\alpha}{\rightarrow} q$ in $N$.  
Thus $q^{'} \overset{w^{R}}{\rightarrow} q$ in $N$ since $w^{R} = \left(\right. a v \left(\left.\right)\right)^{R} = v^{R} a$.

This completes the proof of ($*$).

Then for every word $w \in \left(\Sigma\right)^{*}$ we have

$$
\begin{align}
w \in L \left(\right. M \left.\right) & \iff \exists q \in I_{M} \exists q^{'} \in F_{M} . q \overset{w}{\rightarrow} q^{'} \text{ in } M & & \text{ by definition } \\ 
& \iff \exists q^{'} \in I_{N} \exists q \in F_{N} . q^{'} \overset{w^{R}}{\rightarrow} q \text{ in } N & & \text{ by } \left(\right.*\left.\right) \text{ and } I_{N} = F_{M} , F_{N} = I_{M} \\ 
& \iff w^{R} \in L \left(\right. N \left.\right)
\end{align}
$$
### Closure under Kleene Star
#### Proof using Regular Expressions

Since $L$ is regular, there exists a regular expression $r$ with $L \left(\right. r \left.\right) = L$.

Then $r^{*}$ is a regular expression and hence $L \left(\right. r^{*} \left.\right) = L^{*}$ is a regular language.
